Cook County, Illinois · H.R. 1 (2025)

H.R. 1 could cost up to Cook County residents their Medicaid, SNAP, or both by the end of 2028

This study estimates H.R. 1's effect by comparing Cook County's SNAP caseload with its pre-law trend and projecting Medicaid losses under three scenarios based on past work requirements, then mapping where the losses fall by zip code.

Rushikesh JadhavData through September 30, 2026 (retrieved October 27, 2026)November 2026

On July 4, 2025, President Trump signed H.R. 1, officially the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the large tax and spending law Congress passed that summer.1 Alongside its tax changes, the law cuts federal spending on two programs that millions of low-income Americans rely on: Medicaid, which pays for health care, and the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), which helps families buy groceries.

The biggest change is who gets to keep these benefits. Many adults now have to show, again and again, that they work, train or volunteer about 80 hours a month, or that they qualify for an exemption. SNAP's new rules took effect in Illinois in late 2025. Medicaid's begin in 2027, along with eligibility checks every six months instead of once a year.

In Cook County, these two programs reach more residents than anything else H.R. 1 changes. Medicaid covers adults through the Affordable Care Act expansion,5 and SNAP helped about residents buy groceries in September 2025.3 When people lose either one, many turn to the county: its hospitals, its clinics, and its health plan.

So the county needs answers to three questions: how many residents will lose benefits, where they live, and when. This study answers them with the data available so far.

Why a range? Some of what drives these numbers is not known yet. H.R. 1 requires many adults to show they work, or that they qualify for an exemption, to keep Medicaid and SNAP. In past programs, many people who still qualified lost benefits because they missed that paperwork. How many will miss it in Cook County is not known yet. The low scenario assumes Illinois applies the rules as announced and most people keep up.7 The high scenario assumes losses as large as Arkansas saw in 2018, when it became the first state to add a Medicaid work requirement.11 The middle scenario falls between the two. Together, the three scenarios give this range:

SNAP: the cuts already happened

What changed. SNAP already had a work rule for some adults. Before H.R. 1, adults aged 18 to 54 without children at home could get SNAP for only three months in any three years unless they worked or trained at least 20 hours a week. Veterans, people experiencing homelessness and young adults who had aged out of foster care were exempt, and states could waive the rule in areas with few jobs.1

H.R. 1 raised the age limit to 64 and applied the rule to parents whose youngest child is 14 or older. It removed the exemptions for veterans, homeless people and former foster youth, and made waivers much harder to get. It also ended SNAP for many lawfully present noncitizens, such as refugees and asylees. So the newly affected groups are adults aged 55 to 64, parents of teenagers, veterans, residents without stable housing, and many noncitizens.1,4

Medicaid: the losses are still ahead

Medicaid is public health insurance for people with low incomes. Under the Affordable Care Act expansion, Illinois covers adults aged 19 to 64 with incomes up to 138% of the federal poverty level. In Cook County, adults are covered this way, according to the Illinois Department of Healthcare and Family Services (HFS).5

What changed. Until now, Illinois Medicaid had no work requirement, and adults renewed their coverage once a year. H.R. 1 changes three things for expansion adults:

  • Noncitizens. From October 1, 2026, many lawfully present immigrants, such as refugees and asylees, are no longer eligible.1,9
  • Work requirement. From January 1, 2027, adults must show 80 hours a month of work, job training, school or community service, or qualify for an exemption. Parents of children 13 and under, pregnant women, and people with disabilities or serious medical conditions are among those exempt.1,7 HFS first checks at renewals due in March 2027, so the first coverage losses come in about April 2027.8
  • Renewals every six months. After their first 2027 renewal, expansion adults must renew every six months instead of once a year.1

The people most at risk are adults without young children who work irregular hours or informal jobs, and people who qualify for an exemption but miss the paperwork to prove it. Unlike SNAP, none of this shows up in the data yet, so every Medicaid number below is a projection, not a count.

The money that leaves the county

When residents lose Medicaid or SNAP, the money that paid for their care and groceries stops coming to Cook County. We count it in three separate kinds and never add them together: federal dollars lost, state Medicaid dollars that stop with them, and new costs H.R. 1 shifts onto the state.

Federal dollars. In the middle scenario, Cook County loses about in federal money in 2028 alone: in Medicaid payments for expansion adults' care and in SNAP benefits that would have been spent at local stores. The range runs from to . From 2026 through 2028, the middle scenario adds up to about . The federal government pays 90% of expansion adults' Medicaid costs,1 and all SNAP benefits.

State Medicaid dollars. Illinois pays the other 10% of expansion adults' Medicaid costs. When they lose coverage, that state money stops too: about in 2028 in the middle scenario.

New costs for the state. H.R. 1 also moves SNAP costs from Washington to Illinois. Since October 2026, the state pays 75% of the cost of running SNAP instead of 50%; Cook County's share of that increase is about a year.20 Because Illinois's SNAP payment error rate is high, the state must also pay 15% of SNAP benefits, starting as early as October 2028. If it starts then, Cook's share is about in the last three months of 2028 alone; if Illinois's error rate stays high again, the start moves to October 2029.20 These are costs to the state budget, not the county's.

What it means for county health services. Medicaid pays hospitals and clinics across the county, including the county's own. About of Cook's expansion adults are CountyCare members.16 If they lose coverage at the same rate as everyone else, CountyCare loses about in payments in the county's FY2027 and in FY2028 (middle scenario). The county's own budget already shows the strain: it expects in H.R. 1 fallout for its hospital system in FY2027, charity care rising by , and it has added in local tax support for health care.15

We do not estimate how much more care the county will be asked to provide. The county's budget documents do not separate H.R. 1's share of new costs, and no public data shows where people who lose coverage go for care. What the numbers do show is the gap between the two. A person who loses Medicaid loses a health plan that pays for care anywhere, from a checkup to a prescription. The county can offer care at its own hospitals and clinics, paid from local taxes, once people come in. That is catching up, not replacing what was lost.

What it means for local spending. SNAP benefits are spent at grocery stores in the neighborhoods where people live. Using the U.S. Department of Agriculture's estimate that each $1 of SNAP generates in economic activity,21 the middle scenario's 2028 SNAP losses mean about less local activity and roughly fewer jobs supported. This is a national rule of thumb, so treat it as a sense of scale, not a forecast for Cook County.

What this means for Cook County

By the end of 2028, H.R. 1 could take Medicaid, SNAP or both from between and Cook County residents, about in the middle scenario. The two programs are at different stages. SNAP's losses have already happened: by September 2026, between and fewer residents were getting help with groceries because of the law. Medicaid's losses are still ahead. Apart from a small noncitizen cut in October 2026, they begin in April 2027 and build through 2028.

The losses fall hardest where need is already greatest: Chicago's West and South Sides and the south suburbs. And many of the people who lose Medicaid will still be working. In the middle scenario, at least of them work enough hours and lose coverage only because they could not prove it in time.

Which end of the range Cook County lands on depends mostly on one thing: how many eligible people keep up with the new paperwork. On all five choices Illinois has announced, it picked the most protective option in the Urban Institute's study,7,10 which points toward the low scenario. How people responded to past work requirements points toward the middle and high scenarios.11 These numbers are estimates built from the data available in fall 2026, and they will change as Illinois reports what actually happens.

What these numbers can't tell you

  • Medicaid losses are projections. None of the work-requirement or renewal losses has happened yet. The first real counts will come from HFS after April 2027.
  • The April 2027 SNAP cutoffs are the least certain SNAP number. They are scaled from the 2026 cutoffs, and survey data can only roughly count the adults who face the time limit. Treat that piece as an order of magnitude.
  • We don't follow people after they lose benefits. Some will re-enroll, find job-based coverage or go without. The loss rates already account for people who re-enroll by 2028, but we can't say how many end up uninsured, or for how long.
  • We don't estimate new demand on county services. No public data shows where people who lose coverage go for care, and the county's budget does not separate H.R. 1's share of its new costs.
  • The dollar figures use one average payment. Medicaid dollars use CountyCare's average payment per expansion adult. People who lose coverage may cost more or less than average.
  • Zip numbers are an allocation. County totals are spread across zip areas by where enrollees live today. No agency publishes losses by zip.
  • The SNAP losses are measured against a trend. Other things changed in the same months, including the late-2025 federal shutdown. That is why the low scenario counts only from February 2026, when the time limit legally began.
  • Who loses benefits comes from a survey taken before H.R. 1. It describes people likely to lose benefits, not people counted after losing them.
  • Not included: new applicants turned away by the work requirement; smaller SNAP benefit cuts for people who stay enrolled (utility allowance and internet costs); and H.R. 1's changes to how states finance Medicaid (provider taxes and state-directed payments), which affect hospital revenue directly.
